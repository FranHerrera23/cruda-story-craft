# Nobody ever got fired for buying IBM: why brand matters so much in B2B

Your contact is convinced. They like the proposal, understand the difference and believe your company can solve the problem. Then they take it to a leadership meeting and someone asks: “Who are they? Has anyone worked with them?”

Now they have two things to explain: why the solution fits and why they trust the people delivering it. That second answer puts their own judgment on the line.

“Nobody ever got fired for buying IBM.” The saying captures an advantage that does not appear in a feature comparison: choosing a recognized brand can make a decision easier to defend. **In a significant B2B purchase, brand can act as a shortcut to trust for the person recommending a supplier and for the people who need to support that recommendation.**

## What a familiar brand protects

If a company buys a system that fails, the consequences can reach billing, sales or production. They also reach the person who advocated for the purchase: what they assessed, which risks they saw and why proceeding seemed reasonable.

Peter Weinberg and Jon Lombardo, co-founders of Evidenza, use the IBM saying to discuss the personal and professional risk a brand can help reduce. Their argument concerns the reassurance buyers perceive, not a guarantee that choosing a familiar company protects someone's job. [Weinberg and Lombardo's column](https://www.marketingweek.com/marketing-risk-reduction/).

The distinction matters. A recognized brand lets a conversation begin with some shared familiarity. Other executives may know it, the team may have used its products or there may be references they can consult. The advocate does not need to establish all of the supplier's credibility inside that meeting.

With an unfamiliar company, that work remains. Your contact may be enthusiastic, but everyone else still needs to understand the grounds for their confidence. A sales email can start a valuable relationship. On its own, it leaves questions about responsibility, experience and what happens when delivery becomes difficult unanswered.

## The IBM saying needs closer examination

Taken as a purchasing rule today, the saying would be poor guidance. A familiar company can offer an unsuitable solution, require a disproportionate implementation effort or fall behind a more useful alternative. Keeping a system that obstructs work carries risk too.

AI and new tools broaden the options teams can evaluate. But “it has AI” is not a sufficient reason to choose something either. Buyers need to verify which task improves, what information it requires, how its responses are supervised and what happens when it makes a mistake.

Nor does AI belong exclusively to challengers: both Attio and SAP present AI capabilities within their offerings. The useful comparison concerns what work each solution performs and under which conditions, rather than automatically assigning innovation to newcomers and obsolescence to large companies. [Attio](https://attio.com/) and [SAP ERP](https://www.sap.com/products/erp.html).

Our reading of the saying is more precise: **a recognized reputation can reduce initial uncertainty, but the choice still needs to make sense under scrutiny.** Familiarity creates an advantage. Fit, evidence and terms need to sustain it.

## CRM and email: when the buyer's needs change

A CRM is a system for organizing customer relationships and sales opportunities. In that category, buyers may consider names such as Salesforce or HubSpot and extend their evaluation to alternatives including Attio or HighLevel. Sharing a category does not make every offering interchangeable.

Attio presents a CRM platform with data, automation and AI agents. HighLevel brings CRM, automation and marketing tools together with a proposition particularly oriented toward agencies. These differences give buyers reasons to investigate which fits their team's work. They do not, by themselves, prove that one is taking market share from another. [Attio's offering](https://attio.com/) and [HighLevel CRM](https://www.gohighlevel.com/crm/).

Consider an agency managing several client accounts. Its evaluation may differ from that of a company with sales teams across countries, complex permissions and integrations developed over years. The agency might value a platform combining tasks currently spread across separate tools. The second company also needs to examine what breaks, what must be rebuilt and who supports the migration.

Email offers a similar comparison. Mailchimp provides email, SMS and marketing automation. Beehiiv positions itself around publishing, growing and monetizing newsletters. Kit focuses on creators, with email, automation and digital product sales. These offerings overlap but have different priorities. [Mailchimp](https://mailchimp.com/), [beehiiv](https://www.beehiiv.com/) and [Kit](https://kit.com/).

For someone whose business is a publication, a newsletter-focused tool may be more relevant than one with greater general recognition. A brand less familiar outside that sector may also be well known among their peers. Trust has a context: who recognizes the name, and for solving which problem?

## From a newsletter to the system running operations

Now change the scale. An enterprise resource planning system, or ERP, can connect finance, procurement and supply chains. SAP offers solutions for these operations. Replacing a central part of that structure requires assessing dependencies that a comparison of individual features will not reveal. [Scope of SAP ERP](https://www.sap.com/products/erp.html).

In customer relationship management, Salesforce offers different support and guidance plans. That structure gives buyers something to examine: who helps and under which conditions. The name alone does not establish immediate responses or unlimited coverage; the purchased plan needs reviewing. [Salesforce Success Plans](https://www.salesforce.com/services/success-plans/).

This reveals an understandable reason to choose an established supplier even when another tool appears more agile: buyers need confidence in continuity and the ability to respond. Existing integrations, team knowledge and switching costs can matter too. Attributing every decision to stay to brand strength would be as incomplete as attributing all of them to inertia.

The same reasoning applies beyond software. The following hypothetical examples illustrate purchasing questions, not observed outcomes.

| Situation | What may be at stake | What makes the choice defensible |
|---|---|---|
| Changing newsletter platforms | Delivery, subscriptions and a source of revenue | Tested migration, email delivery and data recovery |
| Changing a sales team's CRM | Opportunity tracking and shared information | Verified integrations, permissions and an adoption plan |
| Replacing a core management system | Billing, procurement and process continuity | Validated migration, accountable owners and a failure response plan |
| Hiring industrial maintenance | Equipment availability and production continuity | Technical capability, coverage, parts and agreed response |
| Choosing a construction supplier | Specification compliance and the sequence of work | Technical documentation, verifiable delivery and clear responsibilities |

*Original comparison by CRUDA. Risk depends on the specific use: a newsletter sustaining a publisher's business can also be critical.*

## How a lesser-known company earns that trust

For a new supplier, the question is what makes recommending it reasonable. Recognizable expertise, a relevant reference or a capability the alternatives lack can give the contact a concrete argument. Evidence needs to match the work being evaluated.

In maintenance, a list of prominent clients may attract interest. A case explaining the equipment serviced, conditions and responsibilities enables a more useful conversation. In construction, “superior quality” leaves unanswered which specification a product meets and who is responsible for delivery. In software, an appealing demonstration still needs checking against the buyer's data and processes.

The initial commitment matters too. A limited pilot can demonstrate capability before expanding a contract, provided it has a scope, an owner and acceptance criteria. Not every purchase allows such a trial; when it does not, references and prior evaluation carry more weight.

Building a brand helps make these signals recognizable before a sales conversation. A company can become known for understanding a specific problem, explaining its decisions and delivering on its promises. Our [B2B differentiation article](/thinking/differentiate-your-b2b-company) explores turning “quality and service” into evidence. Our [AGP sGlass article](/thinking/how-to-get-customers-to-ask-for-your-b2b-brand) examines making a difference visible when other companies sell or integrate the product.

## What your contact takes into the meeting

Return to the person about to recommend you. In our hypothetical industrial maintenance example, operations needs to know how the service fits and what happens after a failure. Procurement needs to compare scope, exclusions and warranties. Finance needs to understand the full cost, the resource commitment and the assumptions supporting the decision.

Doble Calzada Oriente's public procedure makes part of this work visible: its criteria include total cost, delivery, specifications, payment terms, warranty and experience. This is a documented example dated 2021, not a universal rule or confirmation of current practice. [Purchasing procedure, section 5.3.1, in Spanish](https://doblecalzadaoriente.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Procedimiento_contratacion_proveedores.pdf).

The recommendation gains substance when each team can verify what matters to it and identify what remains unresolved. The evidence may support a technical evaluation. It may already support a contract. Authority and the final decision depend on the organization.

The IBM saying remains useful for examining that moment. Your contact brings a proposal, but they are also saying: “I've reviewed this, and I believe we can trust them.” Brand work begins long before that meeting and becomes concrete there: in what others recognize about your company and the reasons they find to support the choice.
